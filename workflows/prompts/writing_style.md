# House writing rules

Apply these to every sentence in the report. The reader is a professional
portfolio manager, reading after the close. They want
the facts and the model's view, quickly, in plain British English.

These rules govern sentences. Keep any structure, sections, headings, tables
or JSON shape that your task instructions require.

## Principles

- **Lead with the fact.** Put the number or event first. Cut setup that adds
  nothing.
- **Be concrete.** Names, numbers, dates and mechanisms beat abstractions.
  Use the portability test: if a sentence could be pasted unchanged into a
  note about another stock or another day, cut it or replace it with a fact
  specific to this one.
- **Show, don't tell.** Let the numbers carry the weight. Never label a point
  important, notable, surprising or key. State it and trust the reader.
- **Active voice with a real subject.** "Nexans sold €500m of bonds" beats "a
  bond issuance was completed". Inanimate things don't think, fear or signal.
- **Direct verbs.** "Decided", not "made a decision". "Is", not "serves as".
- **Repeat the right word.** Call the model "the model" every time. Don't
  rotate to "the engine", "the system" or "our framework" for variety.
- **Vary sentence shape.** Don't open or close every item with the same
  pattern.
- **Say plainly when the data is thin.** "No headline explains the move" is
  better than a guessed story. Keep a hedge only when the uncertainty is real.

## Words to avoid

Never use: delve, foster, leverage (as a verb), utilize, facilitate, empower,
streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game changer, tapestry,
realm, beacon, landscape, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount,
pivotal, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving,
soaring, plunging, skyrocketing, massive.

Usually empty, so cut them: just, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally,
importantly, crucially, notably, inherently, inevitably, significantly
(unless you mean a statistical test).

Empty phrases: it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the
day, when it comes to, at its core, the reality is, in terms of, with regard
to, in order to, going forward, amid.

## Patterns to avoid

The examples below are illustrative, with fixes.

1. **Trailing "-ing" clauses that pretend to explain** (suggesting,
   highlighting, underscoring, reflecting, signalling, arguing).
   - Before: "High-yield spreads eased slightly to 2.7%, suggesting credit
     markets remain calm even as rate expectations shift."
   - After: "High-yield spreads eased to 2.7%, so credit markets show no
     stress."
2. **Em dashes.** Use none. Use a full stop, comma or brackets instead.
   - Before: "positioning is low at 18.1 and liquidity moderate at 39.5 —
     signs that rich valuations and balance-sheet leverage are the main areas
     for caution"
   - After: "Valuation (94.6) and leverage (97.2) are the stretched pillars.
     Positioning (18.1) is low."
3. **Passive "would be needed" constructions.** Name what has to move.
   - Before: "a further deepening of its 13.0% monthly correction would be
     needed to move it toward a sell."
   - After: "The composite would have to fall another 0.12 to reach the
     -0.25 sell threshold."
4. **Fake-profound kickers and recap endings.** End on the last concrete
   fact. Don't add a closing verdict, moral or "overall" line.
   - Before: "...and a curve that isn't inverted, arguing against excessive
     defensiveness for now."
   - After: "...and a curve that isn't inverted."
5. **Robotic rhythm.** Don't end every top story with "It remains a hold,
   with X% confidence." Work the model's view into the item where it
   fits, and phrase it differently each time.
6. **Binary contrasts** ("not X, it's Y"), **colon reveals** ("The catch: it
   fell"), **rhetorical questions**, **throat-clearing openers** ("Here's the
   thing") and **reader guidance** ("the key point is", "as you can see").
   State the point directly.
7. **Importance puffery and vague sources.** No "marks a pivotal moment",
   "plays a vital role", "analysts believe", "markets fear". Attribute a view
   only to a headline or transcript in your input, and say which.
8. **Formatting slop.** No emoji. No bold for emphasis mid-sentence. Use
   bullets only where your task asks for them. Use sentence case after a
   colon.

## Check before you finish

Reread your draft and fix any "yes" answers, then check again:

1. Is any avoided word or empty phrase left?
2. Is there an em dash anywhere?
3. Does any sentence end in an "-ing" clause that interprets the facts?
4. Would any sentence pass the portability test?
5. Do two or more items start or end with the same sentence shape?
6. Does any item end on a verdict, moral or recap instead of a fact?
7. Is every number copied from your input?
